Why Renting Feels Like a Lottery — And How to Improve Your Odds

15 September 2026

If you've applied for a rental lately, you already know the feeling: dozens of strangers attending the open for inspection, possibly a queue stretching out the front door, and that nagging sense that even a great application might not be enough. With rents climbing and vacancy rates stuck near record lows, it's no wonder so many renters describe the process as a bit of a gamble.

We hear it constantly — good tenants putting in application after application, sometimes 20 or 30, without any feedback explaining why they missed out. It's disheartening, and honestly, a lot of the time it really does come down to luck. So let's unpack why that is, and more importantly, what you can actually do about it.

Why the Process Feels So Opaque

Part of the problem is sheer volume. Property managers in busy markets are often fielding an overwhelming number of applications for each home — sometimes so many that even they admit it's impossible to give every applicant personalised feedback. When dozens of renters look almost identical on paper — similar income, similar rental history, nothing that clearly sets one apart from another — agents have told us the decision can come down to something much less tangible: how you presented yourself at the inspection.

That means simple things matter more than you'd think — being on time, being polite, and being genuinely pleasant to the agent showing you through. Some property managers say they specifically remember renters who were short or rude at a busy open home, and that impression can follow an application.

It's also worth knowing that the sense of urgency you feel at a packed inspection isn't accidental. Tenant advocates point out that concentrating viewings into short, busy windows can create pressure that pushes renters to apply quickly — sometimes before they've had a proper chance to look the place over or think through any red flags.

The good news is that some states have started tightening the rules. Victoria, for example, has introduced standardised application forms, some restrictions on the personal information agents can request, and a ban on rent bidding. Queensland and the Northern Territory have gone a step further, explicitly banning landlords and agents from accepting offers above the advertised rent. It's not a perfect system yet, to balance the landlord / tenant dynamic, but it's a sign that reform is possible.

Practical Ways to Improve Your Chances

While you can't control how many other people apply for the same property, there are a few things within your control:

  • Be prepared. Have your rental history, references, proof of income and ID ready to go — the fewer follow-up requests an agent needs to make, the smoother your application looks. Let your employer and reference contacts know to expect an email or call.

  • Be genuinely courteous at inspections. It costs nothing and, it's noticed.

  • Try to build a small rapport. A short, friendly exchange with the leasing consultant can sometimes be the tie-breaker when applications are otherwise indistinguishable.

  • Don't let the pressure rush you. If something about the property doesn't sit right, it's worth taking a pause rather than applying purely because you are getting desperate.

  • Keep a record of what you submit. If you're knocked back repeatedly with no explanation, having a consistent, well-documented application makes it easier to spot if something's going wrong on your end — or if it's simply a numbers game.

Where a Renters' Advocate Comes In

This is where it's worth knowing that you don't have to navigate the system entirely on your own. Renters' advocates and tenant unions exist specifically to help people understand their rights, push back on unfair practices, and make sense of a process that can otherwise feel like a black box.

A good renters' advocate can help you:

  • Understand your rights around application requirements — including what information an agent can and can't legally ask for in your state.

  • Flag discriminatory or unfair practices, such as rental bidding where it's banned, or requests for personal information that go beyond what's reasonable.

  • Advise you on strategies if you are repeatedly rejected, particularly if you suspect discrimination, if you have a pet for example.

  • Advocate for systemic change, pushing for reforms like assessing applicants on their own merits rather than pitting renters against each other in a bidding-war atmosphere.

The Bottom Line

The rental market in all the capital cities right now genuinely is tough, and a lot of what happens behind the scenes is simply out of any individual renter's hands. But presenting yourself well, staying informed about your rights, and knowing there are advocates in your corner can make a real difference — both to your chances of landing a lease, and to how supported you feel while you're searching for one.

You're not imagining it: renting can feel like a lottery. But a little preparation, and the right support, can shift the odds back in your favour.
